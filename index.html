Does sex change what love means? How NU men and women describe the link

PLS 210 · Research Design Part 1 · Fall 2026 · Nazarbayev University · Student pseudonym: X

The puzzle

Research question

In 2024 I interviewed 20 male NU students about sex and love. Almost all said sex can make love stronger. Yet many of the same men said that the more experience they had, the more rarely they felt what they called amorousness. And I had only asked men.

“The feeling stronger than typical sympathy, but not strong enough for love.”

How pilot participants defined amorousness (ANTH 110 pilot, 2024)

How do male and female NU undergraduates describe the link between sexual experience and love in the 2026–2027 academic year?

EmpiricalAsks how people describe something, not how they should live.
ScopedNU undergraduates, compared by gender, at NU in Astana, in 2026–2027.
AnswerableA description is something people can give me in an interview.
Where I stand

Epistemological positioning: interpretivist

Following Schwartz-Shea and Yanow (2012), I want to understand what something means to the people living it, not measure it from outside.

  • The key words have no fixed meaning. “Love” and “amorousness” mean different things to different students; my pilot participants built their own definitions. A 1–10 survey would impose mine, which was my 2024 mistake.
  • I compare meanings, not causes. I ask how men’s and women’s stories differ and overlap, not whether gender causes different feelings.
  • The topic is shaped by shame. What researchers find about double standards depends on how they ask (Crawford & Popp, 2003). Private, open conversations give people room to explain themselves.

I reason abductively: I start from my pilot’s puzzle and let the evidence change my mind. I aim for depth about this group, not generalisation to all of Kazakhstan.

What this adds

Contribution

Research on sexuality in Kazakhstan mostly studies sex education and parents (Kabatova, 2022) or large-scale attitudes (Dall’Agnola & Thibault, 2021). Research on double standards and campus sexuality is mostly American (Crawford & Popp, 2003; Hamilton & Armstrong, 2009). The closest regional study looks at dating in Bishkek (Zhanabayeva, 2018).

Nobody has asked NU undergraduates, men and women, how they connect sexual experience and love. NU is an international, English-speaking university full of students raised under uyat, so it shows where local norms meet global scripts. The study also turns my own flawed comparison into an interpretive one.

What others found

Literature review

Three lines of research, and the useful part is where they pull apart.

Simon & Gagnon1986
Sex follows scripts

Sexuality is learned like a script, at three levels: cultural scenarios (society’s shared instructions), interpersonal scripts (applying them with a partner), and intrapsychic scripts (explaining your own desires).

Why I use it: it explains why my pilot men sounded alike but each told his own story. It is the base of my framework.

Crawford & Popp2003
Gender splits it

A review of twenty years of research: the sexual double standard persists but is subtler and more context-dependent than assumed, and what studies find depends on the method used.

Why I use it: it justifies comparing genders, and asking through open interviews.

Hamilton & Armstrong2009
Gender splits it

A multi-year ethnography of women at a US university. Women faced “double binds”: gender norms disadvantaged them in both hookups and relationships, and the university’s focus on self-development discouraged commitment. Class background changed how they felt this.

Why I use it: a university is not a neutral backdrop. It shapes which options look sensible.

Kabatova2022
Uyat

Uyat undermines young people’s sexual literacy in Kazakhstan. Talking about sex is shameful at home and resisted in schools, and families expect abstinence to protect their honour, differently for girls and boys.

Why I use it: it defines uyat as a regulator of what can be said.

Zhanabayeva2018 · NU thesis
Uyat

Among urban youth in Bishkek, dating is approved as a road to marriage, but premarital sex is condemned for women. Even liberal, educated women feared no longer seeming “pure.” Men face pressure of a different kind.

Why I use it: the closest regional parallel, and a model for interviewing on this topic.

Dall’Agnola & Thibault2021
Kazakhstan today

World Values Survey data plus 96 focus-group participants in five regions: young, urban, single Kazakhs were more exposed online to content promoting divorce and affairs than older, rural, married ones.

Why I use it: NU students live between traditional norms and global online scripts.

Where they disagree. The US campus pushes young people toward casual sex and away from commitment. Central Asian research describes dating as a road to marriage, and women’s sex as a risk to family honour. One review says double standards are getting subtler; the regional studies describe them as strong. Different settings offer different scripts.

The gap. NU sits exactly where these literatures meet, and nobody has asked its students.

My lens

Analytical framework: gendered sexual scripts under uyat

I read students’ descriptions through Simon and Gagnon’s three script levels, with uyat as a local filter on what can be said aloud, by whom, and to whom. These are sensitising concepts: they tell me where to look, not what I will find (Schwartz-Shea & Yanow, 2012).

Cultural scenarioWhat “everyone knows”: “sex should come with love,” “a man needs experience.”
InterpersonalHow students apply or bend those scenarios in their own relationships.
IntrapsychicHow they explain their own feelings, like the “amorousness” in my pilot.
Uyat (filter)When a topic becomes shameful to discuss, and who pays more for speaking.
Double standardThe same experience described as fine for one gender and costly for the other.

Expectations

  • Men will often describe sexual experience as maturity, or as making falling in love rarer.
  • Women will more often describe sex through trust, reputation and future marriage, and speak more carefully.
  • Both will describe a gap between what they believe and what family or “society” expects.
  • How people talk will matter: jokes, silences, or switching from Kazakh to Russian or English may show where uyat works.

I hold this open. Women may describe NU as freer than home; men may feel uyat too. If so, the question can move.

Positionality

I am a male NU undergraduate who has already studied this topic with men. That shapes what people will tell me.

Insider, with men

Shared language and campus life help rapport. But men may perform masculinity for me, by boasting or by sounding “serious.”

Outsider, with women

It is uyat for a young woman to discuss sex with a man. Some may hold back or give “respectable” answers.

What I will do: offer women a female co-interviewer or written or voice-message answers, keep cameras off, and report this effect instead of hiding it. My 2024 conclusions are a bias too, so I will keep memos. New interviews start only after NU ethics (IREC) approval, and I will not ask for partner numbers or health details.

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don’t understand about my research design

What it helped me understand

The difference between asking “does X affect Y?” and “how do people describe X and Y?” My 2024 study was really a positivist design with interpretivist data. And my position as a male researcher is not just a weakness to hide; it shapes the findings and should be reported.

What I still don’t understand

How to compare men and women without slipping back into a two-group, cause-and-effect comparison. How many interviews are enough. Whether a female co-interviewer would make men’s and women’s data too different to compare. And how to treat language switching as evidence rather than noise.
